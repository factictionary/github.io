How to Solve Hard Water Sort & 9x9 Sudoku Logic Puzzles Without Getting Stuck

Understand LIFO stack theory for Water Sort color tubes and learn four intermediate-to-expert Sudoku deduction patterns.

1. The Computer Science Behind Water Sort Puzzles (LIFO Stacks)

At first glance, Water Sort looks like a casual color-matching game. Mathematically, however, every tube in Water Sort is a bounded Last-In, First-Out (LIFO) stack of capacity 4, making the game a close cousin of the Tower of Hanoi and Block-Sorting NP-hard search problems.

When players get stuck after 4 moves, it is almost always because they make "greedy surface merges" (pouring two matching top colors together without checking what lies underneath) instead of optimizing for tube liberation.

Rule #1: Never Occupy Your Last Empty Buffer Tube Without an Exit Plan

Most Water Sort levels give you N colored tubes and 2 empty buffer tubes. Think of those 2 empty tubes as CPU registers: the moment both empty tubes contain different colors, your ability to swap buried colors drops by 80%. Before pouring a new color into your second empty tube, verify that doing so will completely empty another tube within the next 2 to 3 pours.

Rule #2: Scan the Bottom Layer (Depth-4) First

Before making your very first move on a hard level:

  1. Look exclusively at the bottom-most segment (slot 1) of every tube.
  2. If a color (say, Dark Blue) already occupies the bottom slot of 2 or 3 different tubes, do not pour Dark Blue into an empty buffer tube—Instead, work to uncover one of the tubes where Dark Blue is already anchored at the bottom!
  3. Conversely, if a color appears in the top layer (slot 4) of three different tubes and nowhere at the bottom, that color is your prime candidate for your first empty buffer tube because you can immediately stack 3 units of it in one go.

Rule #3: Avoid "Dead Merges"

Suppose Tube A has [Red, Green, Blue, Yellow] (Yellow on top) and Tube B has [Purple, Orange, Pink, Yellow]. Pouring Yellow from Tube A onto Tube B exposes Blue, whereas pouring Yellow from Tube B onto Tube A exposes Pink. Always trace two layers deep: only pour in the direction that unlocks a chain reaction with the other visible top colors.

2. Four Essential 9x9 Sudoku Techniques Beyond Basic Scanning

Most players hit a wall when moving from "Medium" to "Hard" or "Expert" 9×9 Sudoku because basic cross-hatching (scanning rows and columns for single missing numbers) stops revealing cells. Here are the four deductive techniques that unlock 95% of Hard puzzles without guessing:

Technique 1: Snyder Notation (Corner Pencil Marks)

Never clutter a 9×9 grid by writing every possible candidate (1–9) inside every empty square. Instead, use Snyder Notation: within any 3×3 box, only pencil-mark a candidate number if it can go in exactly 2 cells inside that box. The moment you fill one of those two cells with another digit, you instantly know the second cell must contain your penciled number—without re-scanning the row or column.

Technique 2: Pointing Pairs (Box-Line Reduction)

If all possible positions for the number 7 inside Box 1 (top-left 3×3 block) happen to lie in the same horizontal row (Row 2), then even though you don't know which of those two cells in Box 1 is the 7, you know with 100% certainty that 7 cannot appear anywhere else along Row 2 in Boxes 2 and 3.

Technique 3: Naked Pairs & Hidden Pairs

  • Naked Pair: If two cells in the same row, column, or 3×3 box contain the exact same two candidates—say {4, 9} and {4, 9}—then one of them must be 4 and the other must be 9. You can immediately cross out 4 and 9 from all other 7 cells in that unit.
  • Hidden Pair: If candidates 2 and 8 only appear in the same two cells of a row (even if those two cells currently list other pencil marks like {2, 5, 8} and {2, 6, 8}), no other number can occupy those two cells. Erase the 5 and 6 to turn them into a clean Naked Pair {2, 8}.

Technique 4: The X-Wing Pattern

When a single digit (for example, 5) has only two possible spots in Row B and only two possible spots in Row H—and those spots line up in the exact same two vertical columns (say Column 3 and Column 7) forming the four corners of a rectangle—the `5`s must occupy opposite diagonal corners. Consequently, you can eliminate `5` from every other cell in Column 3 and Column 7!
